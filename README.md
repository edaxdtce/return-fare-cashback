# Round trip flights: how to compare return fares across booking sites and earn cashback on the ticket you were buying anyway

Typing "round trip flights" into a search bar usually means one of two things: you have dates and a destination in mind and just want the cheapest return fare, or you have a destination in mind and no idea what the return ticket should cost. Both cases end up in the same place — five browser tabs, three prices for the same seat, and no clear answer about which one is actually cheaper once you factor in everything else.

This piece covers how return fares get priced, when two one-ways beat a round trip, when to book, and where a cashback layer like ShopBack fits without you having to change which airline or booking site you use.

## Why the same return fare shows up at different prices

Google Flights shows you the lowest fare it can find for your dates. It does not show you that the same flight is priced differently on different online travel agencies (OTAs), and it shows no cashback at all. That gap is real: in a three-month test published on ShopBack's travel blog, comparing bookings across sites surfaced price differences of USD 5 to USD 40 per flight on four of six bookings, including a Denver–Cancun round trip quoted at USD 475 to USD 480 depending on the booking site [1].

Travel industry people call this price dispersion. It happens because airlines sell inventory to OTAs at different net rates, and each OTA decides its own margin. It is not a scam, but it is invisible if you only look at one search result page.

## Round trip versus two one-ways

Round trips are not automatically cheaper than two one-way tickets. On a single airline with a return-fare structure, the round trip usually wins. But mixing carriers — one airline out, a different one back — sometimes comes out lower than any single return fare, and it lets you keep the two legs independent if plans change [5]. The tradeoff is that two separate bookings mean two separate change fees, and if the outbound is cancelled, the return ticket does not care.

The practical approach:

- Check the round trip first, since it is one transaction and one set of rules.
- If the return price looks off, price the two legs separately on the same dates.
- Watch connection logic on mixed bookings — if the legs are on separate tickets, a delay on the first does not protect you on the second.

Open-jaw itineraries (fly into one city, fly home from another) are a third option worth checking on trips where you are moving between cities anyway. It is a one-way in each direction, priced separately, often for less than the return to a single city.

## When to book a return flight

There is no universal best day, but there are patterns that hold up well enough to plan around. The booking-window rule most travel agencies quote is 1 to 3 months ahead for domestic flights and 3 to 6 months for international ones; booking much earlier can mean overpaying before sales drop, and booking inside three weeks tends to cost more [6]. For non-peak Southeast Asia routes, ShopBack's own research points to a 4 to 8 week window as the point where fares bottom out, moving to 3 to 5 months out for peak periods and 3 to 6 months for long-haul [5].

On departure days, Tuesday, Wednesday and Saturday are the ones most often cited as cheapest in flexible-date searches, and fares also tend to dip in late January to early March, mid-September to early November, and late April to early May [6].

Price alerts do more for your wallet than daily manual checking. Set them up on your route, then book when the fare drops instead of when the calendar pressures you. If you have already booked and the price falls, the only path back is a rebooking, which usually means eating a change fee — compare the two numbers before assuming a rebook is worth it [5].

## How cashback on flights actually clears

Travel cashback is slow, and that is normal rather than a bug. Cashback moves through four states: tracked, pending, confirmed, withdrawable. Tracking usually shows up within a few hours, often faster, and for flight bookings it typically appears as pending within about 48 hours [12]. The amount then sits pending until the retailer's cancellation window closes — for travel that means after the trip happens, not after you pay [9].

Concretely: flight cashback through a participating OTA commonly confirms 60 to 90 days after the travel date, hotels 30 to 60 days after checkout, activities 15 to 30 days after the activity date, with Skyscanner's store page quoting up to 150 days [5][2]. Pending means the booking was recorded. It is not lost, and nothing you do speeds it up.

If the booking is cancelled, refunded or a no-show, the cashback reverses, because the merchant's commission reverses too. Changing a booking so the booking ID changes can also kill eligibility [2][3].

Withdrawal, once cashback is confirmed, is real money to a bank account or PayPal. In the US, the threshold is $10 of confirmed cashback, withdrawals are capped at $300 per day, and processing typically takes around 10 calendar days, occasionally up to 30 [10].

## A booking workflow that holds up

1. Decide your route and rough dates. If your dates are fully open, use Google Flights' calendar to find the cheap window first.
2. Price the round trip, then price two one-ways and a mixed-carrier return for the same dates.
3. Run the route through Travel Planner, or check the OTA's store page directly, to see the cashback rate attached to each option.
4. Read the exclusions on that store page before booking — eligible booking partners, caps, and anything about packages.
5. Book in the same session you activated. No other coupon sites, no VPN, no ad blocker.
6. Confirm the cashback shows as pending in your account within a couple of days. If a qualifying purchase has not tracked within 48 hours, file a missing cashback claim with the order number, date and amount [10].
7. Leave it. Flight cashback confirms after you fly, not before.

## Questions people ask before booking a return fare

**Is it cheaper to book two one-ways?** Sometimes. It depends on the carriers and the route. Mixed-carrier returns and open-jaw itineraries are worth pricing when your plans are flexible, but two separate tickets mean two sets of change rules.

**Does cashback increase the ticket price?** No. The price shown on the booking site is the same as booking direct; the cashback is funded out of the merchant's affiliate commission, not a markup [12].

**Can I stack it with credit card rewards?** Yes. Card rewards are funded by the issuer and ShopBack cashback by the merchant's commission, so both apply to the same purchase. That is a separate earning layer from whatever cashback the booking site itself offers [5].

**Do airline-direct bookings earn anything?** Usually not. Direct airline bookings are generally not ShopBack partners, which is why routing the same flight through a participating OTA is the standard workaround [8].

**What if I cancel the return leg?** The cashback reverses. Cashback only pays out on completed, non-refunded bookings, and a booking change that alters the booking ID can void eligibility [2][3].
